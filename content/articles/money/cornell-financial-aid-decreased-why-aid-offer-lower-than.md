Cornell does not publish a list of reasons an award shrinks, but its own pages point to five: your family's reported finances changed, your cost of attendance changed, an outside scholarship replaced some of your loans or work-study, federal rules or the FAFSA formula changed, or you fell short on academic progress. Cornell's [FAQ](https://www.finaid.cornell.edu/financial-aid-faq) gives the starting formula: Cost of Attendance minus Expected Family Contribution equals demonstrated financial need. Anything that moves either side moves your aid. The fastest way to find your cause is to compare this year's award letter with last year's, line by line, then ask the aid office about the line that changed.

| Line on your letter that dropped | Most likely cause | Section below |
|---|---|---|
| Cornell grant | Family's reported finances changed, or the FAFSA formula stopped crediting siblings in college | Your family's reported finances changed; The FAFSA formula stopped crediting siblings in college |
| Loan or work-study | An outside scholarship was applied to self-help aid | An outside scholarship arrived |
| Pell Grant | Federal eligibility limits (the Cornell Grant adjusts automatically if Pell changes) | Federal rules changed |
| Cost of attendance | Your need moves with the cost (our reasoning from the need formula) | Step 1 |
| All aid, with a written notice | Satisfactory academic progress | Satisfactory academic progress |
| Aid not renewed or reduced after a missing form | A missed form or deadline | You missed a step or a deadline |
| A new student contribution line | Standard Student Contribution | A standard Student Contribution |

## Step 1: Work out which part of the award dropped

```figure

  - label: Put last year's and this year's award letters side by side
  - label: Compare cost of attendance first, then grants, then loans and work-study
  - label: Match the change to a cause below
  - label: Email or call the aid office with the specific line that changed
  - label: If your family's finances changed, file an appeal with documents
```

1. Compare the **total cost of attendance** on both letters. The FAQ says [your Expected Family Contribution remains consistent unless your family reports significant changes](https://www.finaid.cornell.edu/financial-aid-faq). Our reasoning from the need formula above: if the cost goes up or down, your need and aid move with it.
2. Compare **grants** next. A smaller grant with the same need usually means your family contribution rose. A smaller loan or work-study line with the same grant is a different story (see outside scholarships below).
3. Look for **new items** such as a student contribution or a changed household figure.

## Why aid goes down, by cause

### Your family's reported finances changed

The Cornell aid offer is renewed from forms you file every year. Continuing U.S. students [must complete the CSS Profile to renew the Cornell Aid Offer, and the FAFSA](https://www.finaid.cornell.edu/apply-for-aid/continuing-us-students). A higher parent income, new assets, or a different household situation changes the contribution Cornell expects. Divorced or separated parents add a step: [both biological or adoptive parents must complete separate profiles](https://www.finaid.cornell.edu/apply-for-aid/continuing-us-students).

### The FAFSA formula stopped crediting siblings in college

The Cornell Daily Sun reported in January 2024 that the redesigned FAFSA, which replaced the Expected Family Contribution with the Student Aid Index, [no longer gives families extra eligibility when more than one child is in college](https://cornellsun.com/2024/01/26/new-fafsa-raises-concerns-for-students-with-siblings-in-college-complex-financial-situations/). In that article, Kevin Jensen, executive director of Cornell's Office of Financial Aid and Student Employment, said Cornell meets 100 percent of undergraduates' demonstrated need and that Cornell grant aid would be increased if federal grant aid fell because of the formula changes. That source covers the FAFSA only, and Cornell's pages do not say how siblings affect the CSS Profile calculation, so ask the aid office how your household figures were counted.

### An outside scholarship arrived

This one can look like a cut when it is not a cost to you. Per the [FAQ](https://www.finaid.cornell.edu/financial-aid-faq), outside scholarships reduce self-help aid, meaning student loans or work-study, dollar for dollar. Cornell may occasionally need to reduce federal, state or other grants to comply with applicable rules. The FAQ asks you to upload your scholarship award notification to the Secure Document Upload portal. If your loan line shrank and your grant did not, check here first.

### Federal rules changed

Cornell's [federal changes page](https://finaid.cornell.edu/federal-financial-aid-changes) lists these Pell limits for 2026-27: a maximum Pell of $7,395, ineligibility for students with a Student Aid Index of $14,790 or more (twice the maximum Pell), and ineligibility for students whose non-federal grants and scholarships equal or exceed their total cost of attendance. Cornell adds that Cornell Grant-eligible students are not affected, and that [if Pell eligibility changes, the Cornell Grant adjusts automatically](https://finaid.cornell.edu/federal-financial-aid-changes). So a lost Pell grant should show up as a larger Cornell grant, not a bigger hole. If you see the Pell line fall with no matching rise, ask.

The same page covers loan changes that start July 1, 2026. Parent PLUS limits are now [$20,000 per year and $65,000 lifetime, per student](https://finaid.cornell.edu/federal-financial-aid-changes). Undergraduate loan limits are unchanged.

### You missed a step or a deadline

Missing paperwork is one cause of lost upperclassman aid that [uAspire describes](https://www.uaspire.org/News-Events/Why-Upperclassmen-Lose-Financial-Aid), which says upperclassmen may have to file several forms with different due dates. This is a general national source, not a Cornell one. For 2027-28, Cornell asks continuing students to file the CSS Profile [as soon as possible after October 1, 2026 and no later than February 2, 2027](https://www.finaid.cornell.edu/apply-for-aid/continuing-us-students), and the FAFSA as soon as possible after October 1, 2026. The page does not say what a late filing costs, so do not assume there is a grace period. Also answer every document request: Cornell's page says to [avoid uploading documents that have not been requested](https://www.finaid.cornell.edu/apply-for-aid/continuing-us-students).

### Satisfactory academic progress

Federal aid requires it, and Cornell [evaluates it annually, after final grades post](https://finaid.cornell.edu/federal-satisfactory-academic-progress). For undergraduates the standards are:

| Standard | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Completion rate | At least 67% of attempted credits |
| Cumulative GPA | At least 2.0 |
| Maximum timeframe | 150% of the program's published credits (180 attempted credits for a 120-credit degree) |

Attempted credits include withdrawals, failures, incompletes and repeated courses. The consequence is [loss of financial aid eligibility, with written notification](https://finaid.cornell.edu/federal-satisfactory-academic-progress) from the office. Paying for classes or sitting out a semester does not restore eligibility. You can appeal, and an approved appeal gives one semester of financial aid probation.

### A standard Student Contribution

The FAQ refers to a [standard Student Contribution](https://www.finaid.cornell.edu/financial-aid-faq) that students are expected to earn and save, mainly over the summer. If you cannot earn and save it, the FAQ says you may request a loan to replace that amount through the Undergraduate Student Appeal Form.

## Step 2: Ask the aid office the right question

The office is reachable at [607-255-5145 or finaid@cornell.edu](https://finaid.cornell.edu/special-circumstances), and you can schedule a counselor appointment online. The [special circumstances page](https://finaid.cornell.edu/special-circumstances) lists virtual appointment hours Monday to Friday, 10:00am to 1:00pm and 2:00pm to 4:00pm. Specific questions get specific answers:

- "Which line of my award changed, and what input on my CSS Profile or FAFSA drove it?"
- "Did my cost of attendance change, or my family contribution?"
- "Is an outside scholarship being applied to loans, work-study or a grant?"
- "Is a Pell or New York State change being offset by a Cornell Grant adjustment?"
- "How were my siblings counted in my CSS Profile calculation?"

## Step 3: Appeal if your finances changed

A [special circumstances appeal](https://finaid.cornell.edu/special-circumstances) asks for a lower expected family contribution based on changes since you applied or on unusual family situations. It needs supporting documentation and is decided case by case. Cornell's [appeals page](https://finaid.cornell.edu/node/41699) gives a typical response time of 10 to 15 business days, reviews appeals in the order received, and cautions that approved appeals may not result in additional grant or scholarship aid. It also says Cornell offers no merit aid and does not negotiate offers, so an appeal is not a bargaining chance. See [Cornell financial aid appeals: what works, by situation](/money/cornell-financial-aid-appeal-success/) for how to build one.

## Common mistakes

Our advice, drawn from the rules above:

- Comparing only the bottom-line number. A shrinking loan line and a shrinking grant line have different causes.
- Waiting until the February 2, 2027 CSS Profile deadline. Cornell asks you to file soon after October 1, and it asks for the CSS Profile by February 2, 2027.
- Skipping the outside scholarship upload. Cornell only knows what you report.
- Treating an appeal as a negotiation. It is for changed circumstances with documents.

## FAQ

**Does Cornell meet full need every year?** The Daily Sun quotes the executive director of the aid office saying Cornell [meets 100 percent of undergraduates' demonstrated financial need](https://cornellsun.com/2024/01/26/new-fafsa-raises-concerns-for-students-with-siblings-in-college-complex-financial-situations/). The amount you are expected to pay can still change.

**Does aid decrease automatically for upperclassmen?** Cornell's pages do not say so. A national [uAspire article](https://www.uaspire.org/News-Events/Why-Upperclassmen-Lose-Financial-Aid) describes schools that front-load awards, but it does not name Cornell, and Cornell's formula is need-based.

**Do I need to reapply every year?** Yes. Continuing students file the [CSS Profile and FAFSA each year](https://www.finaid.cornell.edu/apply-for-aid/continuing-us-students).

**Can I change my mind on an outside scholarship?** The FAQ does not say. Ask the aid office before you accept one if the loan reduction matters to you.
