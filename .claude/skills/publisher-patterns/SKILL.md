# Publisher patterns

Readers already know how good explainers feel because they read Investopedia, Niche, the Times, CNN and Fox. EduExplained should feel at least that easy to use, with more depth and better data. This skill distills what those publishers do into rules you can apply. Never copy their wording, headlines or page text; take the patterns only.

## The seven patterns every top explainer shares
1. **The answer lives above the fold.** Every one of them puts a summary before the body: Investopedia's "Key Takeaways," Fox's "The Brief," Credible's takeaways box, the TV-news dek under the headline. A reader who stops after 10 seconds still leaves with the answer.
2. **Headlines say exactly what the page answers.** Formulas that recur: "What Is X? Definition, How It Works, and Types" (Investopedia), "X: Here's what to know" (CNN), "What borrowers and parents need to know about..." (Fox). The headline promises one job and the page does it.
3. **Segmenting by situation.** The best news explainers organize by who the reader is ("If you were enrolled in the SAVE plan," "If you borrowed on or after July 1, 2026"). This is our reader-block idea, but used as the main structure when situations drive the answer.
4. **Labeled scan sections.** Fox's local explainers label mini-sections inside each heading: "What we know," "By the numbers," "Why you should care," and end with "The Source." Labels let a reader jump to the kind of information they want.
5. **Numbers get their own space.** Limits, rates and caps go into short lists or tables, never buried mid-paragraph. A three-line list of borrowing caps beats a sentence holding all three.
6. **Visible trust signals.** Investopedia shows the author, "Updated" date, "Reviewed by" a credentialed expert and "Fact checked by" on every page, plus a public editorial policy. Trust is designed, not assumed.
7. **The page leads somewhere next.** Series links, related terms, "Key articles," tools and calculators keep readers moving through the site. Investopedia's 14,000+ term definitions all link into each other.

The full anatomy of each publisher, with what we adopt and reject, is in `references/publisher-anatomy.md`. Read it when you're designing a page type for the first time or when a page feels hard to scan.

## What EduExplained adopts
| Pattern | Our version |
|---|---|
| Summary box | **Key takeaways:** 3 or 4 bullets, each a decision or a number, directly under the headline. It counts as each fact's one allowed restatement. |
| Dek | One sentence under the headline that names who the page is for and the biggest thing to know. |
| Definition first (Investopedia) | For explainer pages, the first sentence defines the term in plain words. Jargon gets defined in a few words on first use, every time. |
| Situation segmenting (CNN, AP) | When the answer depends on who you are, sections are "If you're a transfer student" etc. Otherwise use reader blocks. |
| Labeled scan sections (Fox) | Optional labels inside long sections: **By the numbers**, **What changes for you**, **Watch out**. Use them where they help scanning, never as decoration. |
| Numbers in lists and tables | Any section with 3 or more numbers presents them as a list or table. |
| Calculators (NYT Upshot) | Where the answer depends on the reader's own numbers (rent split, break-even, aid estimate), the layering agent asks for a tool. Tools are our most engaging and most-linked asset. |
| Trust signals (Investopedia) | "Updated" date, real author byline, sources list, editorial standards page. Never invent reviewers or credentials. |
| Next step (all) | Every page ends with a "What to do next" step and a related-guides module. |
| Data report cards (Niche) | For data pages: a top band of 4 to 6 key stats with the year and source, then the verdict. Grades only when we publish the method. |

## What EduExplained rejects
- **Editorializing in news framing.** Some news explainers slip opinion into factual sentences ("taxpayers will no longer foot the bill," "a lot to unpack"). We state facts, attribute disputes, and keep our advice clearly labeled as advice.
- **Quote-driven filler.** News pieces often pad with expert quotes that restate the point ("First and foremost, find out what you have"). We give the instruction directly.
- **Anecdote openings.** "UNLV graduate student X says..." openings delay the answer. Real examples come after the answer, if at all.
- **Generic SEO-writer advice.** Popular community "SEO content writer" skills on GitHub recommend fixed keyword density, a "hook" intro, word-count targets and a "conclusion with CTA." All four work against us: they produce padded, generic pages. Our rules (answer first, coverage over length, each fact once) replace them.
- **User reviews as content.** Niche relies heavily on student reviews; we never use forum or review posts as sources.
