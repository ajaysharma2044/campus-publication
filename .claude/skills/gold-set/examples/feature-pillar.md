## Why this works, pattern by pattern

**1. A scene that creates tension, then the frame, then the stakes.**
The lede puts the reader in a specific, slightly absurd moment: undergraduates signing, every October, a lease for an apartment they won't enter for nearly a year, some before they know their roommates, some for buildings not yet finished. The second paragraph names the frame for the whole page: a two-tier system with a hard seam down the middle (university-controlled first two years, one of the tightest small-city rental markets after). The third gives the stakes in numbers ($21,348 average housing and dining, $73,946 tuition) and promises the page's path ("every rung of that ladder"). Three paragraphs, and the reader knows why to keep going and what they'll get.

**2. A story spine the reader already lives.**
Sections follow the student's own path: Year One, Year Two, program houses, chapter houses and co-ops, Collegetown, graduate students, prices, special cases, the bigger picture, the calendar. A reader can jump to "where I am now" and the whole page still reads as one story.

**3. Specific names everywhere.**
Hall names with opening years and bed counts, the pronunciation and meaning of Ganędagǫ:, the addresses of affiliated houses, the five West Campus houses by name, The Ivy at Dryden Road and College Avenue, 301 College Avenue bought out of foreclosure for $15 million. Nothing is "a residence hall" or "a new building" when it could be named.

**4. Why things are the way they are.**
The sophomore requirement is explained with its date (fall 2022), its announcement (April 2021), what made it possible (about 800 new beds earmarked for sophomores), the university's argument (academic achievement, food security) and the cost to students who'd left to cook for themselves. Fraternity rules trace back to the 2018 reforms. The city's rent rise is tied to university housing policy and enrollment growth. This context is the new information: it exists on a dozen separate pages and nowhere together.

**5. Current events woven in, not bolted on.**
The October 2026 suspension of fraternity social events, the city's moratorium on student event permits, 301 College Avenue opening half-renovated: each appears where it matters to a housing decision ("any student planning to live in one for 2027-28 should watch that review closely").

**6. Our own math as insight.**
Room plus Unlimited meal plan plus the dining fee comes to $21,398, almost exactly the trustees' $21,348 average. A $1,615 Collegetown lease over 12 months is $19,380 against $13,744 for a campus double, which is why "the comparison is rarely as favorable to off-campus living as students assume." Arithmetic turned into a finding.

**7. Fair trade-offs from reputable reporting.**
Early residents of the new suite halls liked the space and air conditioning but found the closed suite doors less social than corridor dorms. Critics argued the expansion would push roughly 1,000 to 1,100 more upperclassmen into Collegetown. The page reports both sides of real debates without editorializing.

**8. Reporting-style attribution.**
Short parenthetical source names after facts: (Cornell Chronicle), (Cornell Housing rates), (Cornell Daily Sun). Every table carries a source line underneath. No "we found," no "per X's page read on Nov 8."

**9. Tables for reference, prose for meaning.**
Bed counts, program houses and their themes, co-op rates, Hasbrouck rents, room rates and the housing calendar are all tables. The interpretation of those numbers is always prose.

**10. It ends forward, then turns to service.**
"The Bigger Picture" closes on three concrete open questions about the future. Then the page shifts to tools: a dated housing calendar, a "before you sign" checklist and the sources list.
